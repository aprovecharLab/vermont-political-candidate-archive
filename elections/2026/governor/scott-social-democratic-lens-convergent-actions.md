# Governor Phil Scott's Record Through a Social Democratic Lens: Convergent Actions (2017–2026)

## Introduction

This companion document to "Governor Phil Scott's Veto and Let-Pass Record Through a Social Democratic Lens" examines areas where Governor Phil Scott's actions converged with social democratic priorities — what he signed into law, what he advocated for, and where his governing record aligned (at least partially) with the four organizations used as analytical lenses: the Democratic Socialists of America (DSA), Social Democrats of America (SDA), the Socialist International (SI), and Social Democrats USA (SDUSA).

Between January 2017 and August 2026, Governor Scott signed hundreds of bills into law. This analysis isolates the subset most relevant to social democratic commitments, graded by evidence strength: **Tier 1 (Initiated/Advocated)** — bills or policies he proposed or publicly championed; **Tier 2 (Signed with Advocacy)** — bills he signed while expressing support; **Tier 3 (Permitted Without Objection)** — legislation he allowed to proceed without veto despite potential for opposition.

**Critical Methodological Note:** Where this document claims convergence with social-democratic tenets, it reflects Scott's *actions*, not his *ideology*. A conservative governor who signs progressive legislation for political or pragmatic reasons does not thereby become a social democrat. The analysis below grades policy alignment, not philosophical conversion — an honest accounting requires distinguishing what Scott *did* from what he *said*.

## Thematic Analysis of Convergent Actions

### Gun Safety and Civil Liberties

Scott's most substantively progressive record concerns firearm regulation — a surprising domain of convergence for a Republican governor. In March 2018, following the Parkland shooting, he signed three bills that fundamentally reshaped Vermont's approach to gun violence:

- Raised the minimum age to purchase firearms to 21
- Mandated background checks for all private sales (including at gun shows)
- Created Extreme Risk Protection Orders (ERPOs/"red flag" laws)

At the signing ceremony, Scott stated: *"This is not the time to do what's easy, it's time to do what's right."*

This represented Tier 2 action (signed with advocacy). None of the four organizations treats gun regulation as a core plank, but SDUSA and SI both emphasize public safety and civil liberties frameworks that accommodate such measures. His 2023 let-pass of H.230 (which added a 72-hour waiting period despite constitutional doubts) and his 2024 let-pass of S.209 (ghost gun ban) reinforced this pattern.

### Consumer Privacy and Digital Rights

In June 2026, Scott signed two major privacy measures:

- **S.71 (Vermont Data Privacy and Online Surveillance Act)** — Vermont became the 24th state to enact comprehensive consumer data privacy, granting residents rights to confirm processing, delete data, and opt out of sales of sensitive information. Effective January 1, 2028.
- **H.211 (Data Broker and Personal Information Act)** — Amended Vermont's 2018 pioneering data-broker registration law, raising fees to $900 annually, requiring a $20,000 surety bond, adding consumer deletion rights, and strengthening enforcement. Effective January 1, 2027.
- **H.639 (Genetic Data Privacy)** — Signed June 2026, establishing genetic privacy protections.
- **H.814 (Neurological Rights and AI)** — Signed May 2026, protecting neurological data and regulating AI in healthcare.

These are Tier 2 actions. While DSA and SDUSA do not foreground digital privacy, the SI declaration's emphasis on human rights and SDUSA's civil-liberties commitments create room for alignment. These bills make Vermont a national leader in data protection — a significant institutional achievement.

### Healthcare: Corporate Accountability and Systemic Reform

Scott's healthcare record is mixed, but one signature stands out:

- **Private Equity Restrictions (June 2026)** — Signed legislation prohibiting corporate entities (private-equity firms, hedge funds) from interfering in medical practice decisions at facilities they own, including pricing, staffing, and equipment purchases. Vermont became only the third state to attempt this restriction.

Scott's stated rationale focused on preserving physician autonomy and patient care quality. The Washington Post opined against the bill, arguing it "is regulating a largely hypothetical problem in a way that could make health care worse" — underscoring that this was an unusual Republican action.

This qualifies as Tier 2 (signed with advocacy). While far short of the universal healthcare demanded by DSA, SDA, SI, and SDUSA, this represents genuine skepticism of financialization in essential services — a position that overlaps with social democratic concerns about corporate control of public goods.

Additionally, Scott announced in December 2022 the launch of a **voluntary paid family and medical leave program**, to be operational by 2025. This is a Tier 1 action (administration-initiated), though its voluntary nature distinguishes it from the mandatory, universal programs all four organizations would prefer.

### Housing and Homelessness Response

Scott's housing record combines funding commitments with deregulatory impulses:

- **Vermont Housing and Conservation Board (VHCB)**: Fully funded at $37 million annually in his budgets
- **Vermont Housing Improvement Program (VHIP)**: Ongoing funding to accelerate affordable unit production
- **Total Housing Investment**: Nearly $800 million since 2020 per his 2026 budget address
- **General Assistance Emergency Housing Executive Order (EO 03-25, March 2025)**: Directed state agencies to prioritize emergency shelter funding
- **SNAP Legal Action (October 2025)**: Scott joined other governors in contemplating litigation against the Trump administration over federal cuts to food assistance

The VHCB and VHIP commitments are Tier 2 (budget-funded). The SNAP legal stance is Tier 1 (administration-led). His 2025 executive order on emergency housing (signed alongside climate-related directives) signals willingness to deploy executive power for shelter access.

However, Scott simultaneously vetoed the childcare payroll tax (H.217, 2023 — overridden to become Act 76) and multiple property-tax-based education finance bills, signaling fiscal caution that undercuts social-democratic expansionary ambitions. The housing record is therefore partial convergence: he invested in supply-side solutions while resisting tax-based redistribution mechanisms.

### Climate and Environment: Executive Action vs. Legislative Opposition

This category reveals the clearest divergence between Scott's executive actions and legislative vetoes. His executive orders signal some environmental commitment:

- **EO 04-25 (May 2025)**: Advanced Clean Trucks, Advanced Clean Cars II, and Heavy-Duty Engine/Vehicle Omnibus standards
- **EO 03-25 (March 2025)**: General Assistance Emergency Housing (also homelessness response)

These are Tier 1 actions (his administration initiated them). They represent regulatory modernization consistent with climate goals, though they stopped short of mandating the structural transitions demanded by DSA's Green New Deal planks or SI's climate-justice framework.

The record must be read holistically: Scott vetoed the Global Warming Solutions Act (2020), Clean Heat Standard (2022), Affordable Heat Act (2023), and Renewable Energy Standard (2024), all of which were subsequently overridden. He signed the Climate Superfund Act (S.259, 2024) without his signature while expressing concern about litigation risks. The executive orders, then, operate within a narrower band than social democrats would prefer — administrative adjustments rather than economic restructuring.

### Labor and Workplace Protections: Mixed Record

This is Scott's weakest area of convergence. Two items warrant mention:

- **Return-to-Office Mandate (2025)**: Scott required state employees to return to physical offices three days per week. The Vermont Labor Relations Board ordered him to rescind the order, ruling it violated collective-bargaining agreements and interfered with union rights. The state was ordered to rehire employees who resigned and compensate monetary losses. This is a negative example — a failure.
- **Voluntary PFML Program (December 2022)**: As noted above, this Tier 1 announcement precedes a 2025 launch. It is voluntary and insurance-based rather than mandatory and universal.

Neither meets social democratic standards. The PFML program is better understood as a compromise position — acknowledging worker needs while avoiding the mandatory funding mechanisms Scott repeatedly vetoed.

### Democracy and Civic Integrity

Scott demonstrated occasional commitment to transparency and anti-corruption measures:

- **Ethics Commission Strengthening (H.875, 2024)**: Allowed to become law without signature, updating the State Code of Ethics
- **Truth and Reconciliation Commission (H.649, 2023)**: Allowed to become law without signature despite concerns about private deliberations
- **SNAP Legal Action (October 2025)**: Defending federal benefits against federal cuts

The SNAP action is Tier 1 (administration-led). The let-passes of ethics bills are Tier 3 (permitted). These align somewhat with SDUSA's procedural-democratic commitments and SI's transparency principles, though neither represents transformative reform.

### Immigration and Refugee Support

Scott's record is defensive rather than expansionary:

- **SNAP Legal Action**: Protecting benefits that serve immigrant families among other vulnerable populations
- **No major pro-immigrant legislation signed**: No sanctuary-city mandates, no path-to-status bills on his desk to sign
- **H.849 let-pass (Act 87, 2026)**: Enabled civil suits against government officials for federal constitutional rights violations. This is Tier 3 evidence (permitted, not advocated): Scott allowed it to become law while stating it risked giving Vermonters "false hope" that it would survive court challenges, permitting it only after the Attorney General advised it was defensible. It registers as passive tolerance, not championing — the legislature and AG, not the governor, deserve the credit.

This is Tier 2/3 territory (limited convergence). All four organizations advocate robust immigrant rights; Scott's record shows passive tolerance rather than active advocacy.

### Childcare: What Actually Happened

This requires explicit correction. Aly Richards' campaign highlighted Act 76 (universal childcare funded by a payroll tax) as her signature achievement. The historical record:

- **Scott vetoed H.217** on June 6, 2023, stating: "I'm not vetoing childcare. I'm vetoing the payroll tax."
- **The legislature overrode his veto** on June 20, 2023 (House 116–31, Senate sufficient margin)
- **Act 76 became law without his support**

This is not a positive example for Scott — it is a case where progressive policy advanced *despite* him, not through him. Richards' campaign framing obscures this distinction. Social democrats should credit the legislature and advocates, not the governor.

## Alignment with the Four Organizations: Convergent Record

### Democratic Socialists of America (DSA)

**Partial Alignment (Weak)**: Scott's gun-safety signatures (2018), data-privacy laws (2026), and private-equity healthcare restrictions (2026) represent genuine policy achievements that overlap with DSA's public-safety and anti-corporate-control planks. His voluntary PFML program acknowledges worker-protective needs without embracing the mandatory universal model DSA demands. However, his systematic veto of labor bills, minimum-wage increases, climate mandates, and the childcare payroll tax — all overridden — means his convergent actions are outnumbered and undercut by his obstructionist record.

### Social Democrats of America (SDA)

**Partial Alignment (Weak)**: SDA's platform emphasizes universal healthcare, education, reproductive rights, and worker rights. Scott signed the private-equity healthcare restrictions and the data-privacy laws — both compatible with SDA's consumer-protection and civil-liberties instincts. He permitted universal school meals (H.165) and the Baby Bond Trust (H.55) to become law unsigned. However, his labor vetoes, PFML voluntariness, and climate-filed objections mean this is partial, not robust, alignment. His 2018 gun-safety package remains the single strongest convergent action across any organizational rubric.

### Socialist International (SI)

**Partial Alignment (Weak)**: SI's Declaration of Principles calls for public ownership, participatory democracy, climate justice, and international solidarity. Scott's data-privacy and neurological-rights bills echo SI's human-rights commitments. His SN APP legal action reflects international-solodarity logic. His executive orders on clean vehicles show administrative climate action. But SI's emphasis on public ownership and participatory democracy finds no strong correlate in Scott's record — his let-passes of transparency bills are minor, and his climate vetoes directly contradict SI's climate-justice plank.

### Social Democrats USA (SDUSA)

**Moderate Alignment (Best Fit)**: SDUSA's reformist, mixed-economy framework accommodates Scott's greatest achievements: the gun-safety package, data-privacy laws, private-equity restrictions, housing investments, and fiscal caution paired with targeted public spending. His approach — pragmatic legislative action within existing institutions, avoiding revolutionary transformation — matches SDUSA's incrementalism. However, even SDUSA supports a living minimum wage, union power, and Green New Deal-type programs, all of which Scott opposed. On the SDUSA rubric, he registers as a "partial partner on consumer protection and public safety, adversary on labor and climate."

## Key Observations

**1. Scott's convergent record is real but narrower than his divergent record.** Seven to nine substantive bills or executive actions across five terms converge meaningfully with social democratic tenets. By contrast, the veto document identified twenty-plus major bills where Scott opposed social-democratic priorities. The ratio favors divergence.

**2. Most convergence is consumer protection, not economic restructuring.** Scott's privacy laws, data-broker rules, and private-equity restrictions are all about regulating market actors — not transforming ownership structures or expanding public provision. This matches SDUSA's regulatory-reform model, not DSA's transformative vision.

**3. His strongest positive actions predate the current political moment.** The 2018 gun-safety package was a national outlier in its ambition and Scott's advocacy. The 2026 privacy bills followed years of legislative groundwork. Meanwhile, labor and climate — central to the 2020s social-democratic agenda — saw systematic veto resistance.

**4. Executive orders fill gaps where legislation failed.** Scott used executive power on climate (clean trucks/cars), emergency housing, and pandemic-era childcare — areas where legislative proposals were blocked or stalled. This is administratively creative but politically constrained: EO-based policy is easier to reverse and lacks statutory permanence.

**5. The childcare story requires clear attribution.** Act 76 passed *over* Scott's veto, not through his support. Any document crediting him with childcare achievement misrepresents the record. The correct narrative: Vermont's universal childcare law was a legislative victory achieved despite gubernatorial opposition.

**6. Evidence tiers matter.** This document's three-tier grading exposes important distinctions. Scott initiated very little Tier 1 policy (SNAP legal action, PFML announcement, climate EOs). Most of his convergent actions were Tier 2 (signed with advocacy, gun safety, privacy, private equity) or Tier 3 (let-passes, minimal obstructions). Compare this to the veto document, where Scott's *opposition* was unambiguously Tier 1 (active vetoes). The asymmetry itself tells a story: he obstructed more aggressively than he constructed.

## Addendum: Disclaimer on Data Uncertainty

*Note: This analysis inherits the data limitations of its source compilations. Several 2018 and 2022 veto reasons remain unrecoverable, and some let-pass letters were not fully retrievable. The "convergent" examples listed here reflect bills where Scott's public statements or press releases expressed support, but for many others his reasoning is inferred from silence rather than documented. The approximate totals (~7–9 convergent actions vs. ~20+ divergent actions) are interpretive summaries, not neutral facts. Readers should verify individual bill claims against the Vermont Legislature's official trackers before citing specific examples.*

*Importantly, this document does not claim Scott acted from social-democratic motives — only that his actions in certain domains aligned (partially and selectively) with social-democratic priorities. Attribution of motive is beyond the scope of documentary analysis.*
