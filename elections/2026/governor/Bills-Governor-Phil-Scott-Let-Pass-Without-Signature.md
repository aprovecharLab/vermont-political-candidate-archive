# Bills Governor Phil Scott Let Pass Without His Signature (2017–Present)

Vermont governors serve two-year terms. Phil Scott took office in January 2017 and was reelected to a fifth term in 2024. Under Vermont law, if the governor takes no action on a bill for five days (excluding Sundays) after receiving it while the legislature is in session, the bill becomes law without their signature. Below is every bill identified that Scott allowed to become law without signing, organized by term and year, with descriptions and known reasons.

## Summary of Recurring Themes

Scott's reasons for withholding his signature generally fall into several categories:

- **Constitutional concerns** — He flagged provisions he believed might not survive legal scrutiny (e.g., waiting periods for gun purchases, civil action against federal officials) and chose to let the courts resolve them rather than blocking the bill outright.
- **Policy disagreement without opposition to intent** — Many bills (cannabis regulation, climate superfund, employment protections) aligned partially with his views but went further than he preferred; he signaled disapproval without escalating to a veto.
- **Ranked-choice voting** — He consistently refused to sign charter amendments enabling ranked-choice voting, stating his philosophical position that "one person should get one vote, and candidates who get the most votes should win elections."
- **Open government transparency** — He withheld his signature from bills that created exceptions to Vermont's Open Meeting Law (Truth and Reconciliation Commission, Ethics Commission).
- **Avoiding government shutdowns** — In budget disputes, he allowed budgets to pass unsigned when the alternative was a shutdown, even when he found the spending plan unsatisfactory.
