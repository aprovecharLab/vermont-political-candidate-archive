# Governor Phil Scott's Veto and Let-Pass Record Through a Social Democratic Lens (2017–2026)

## Introduction

Incumbent Republican Governor Phil Scott enters the 2026 general election seeking a sixth term, facing Amanda Janoo — who campaigned on a platform with meaningful overlap with social democratic traditions (as did her primary opponent Ali Richards as was analyzed in the companion primary document). This analysis extends that same framework to the incumbent, but with a critical methodological difference: rather than assessing campaign platforms, it assesses **revealed governing behavior** — what Governor Scott actively blocked (vetoes), what he allowed to become law while distancing himself from it (let-passes), and the reasons he gave.

Between January 2017 and August 2026, Scott vetoed approximately 66 bills and allowed roughly 28 more to become law without his signature, breaking Howard Dean's all-time Vermont veto record in June 2021. His veto reasons cluster around affordability and cost concerns, separation of powers, housing and land use, and environmental and energy policy — each of which intersects directly with core social democratic commitments.

This analysis measures Scott's record against the same four organizations used in the primary comparison: the Democratic Socialists of America (DSA), Social Democrats of America (SDA), the Socialist International (SI), and Social Democrats USA (SDUSA). As with the candidates, this examines policy overlap, not endorsements or affiliations — Governor Scott is affiliated with none of these organizations, and this lens is intentionally one-directional: it asks how close his *actions* came to their stated tenets, knowing they are the measuring stick rather than a neutral one.

---

## Important Methodological Caveats

Three caveats should govern any reading of this document:

**1. Vetoes measure distance from a legislature, not distance from social democracy.** Vermont's legislature has been dominated by a Democratic/Progressive supermajority for most of Scott's tenure. His vetoes therefore show what he blocked from a consistently left-leaning body — a record of friction with social-democratic *outputs*, which is not identical to a record of opposition to social-democratic *principles*. A governor in a right-leaning state with an identical ideology would produce almost no comparable data.

**2. Let-passes complicate the picture.** Scott frequently declined to block progressive legislation outright, allowing universal school meals (H.165), the Climate Superfund Act (S.259), gun safety measures including waiting periods (H.230), the ghost gun ban (S.209), and expanded employment protections (S.102) to become law over his stated objections. A uniformly antagonistic executive would have vetoed these. His pattern — veto where cost or separation-of-powers concerns are implicated, let-pass where constitutional doubt can be deferred to courts or where the political cost of a veto is prohibitive — is one of selective obstruction, not total opposition.

**3. Revealed behavior understates areas of silence.** The veto record tells us little about domains where the legislature did not send him social-democratic legislation. Absence of data is not evidence of position.

## Thematic Analysis of the Record

### Labor, Worker Rights, and Wages

This is Scott's densest record of divergence from all four organizations. Across three terms he vetoed:

- **Paid family and medical leave** — twice (H.196, 2018; H.107, 2020)
- **Minimum wage increases** — twice (S.40, 2018; S.23, 2020)
- **Collective bargaining** (S.125, 2025)
- **Fair employment practices** (S.230, 2026)
- **Employment protections and collective bargaining expansion** (S.102, 2024 — let-pass rather than veto)

SDUSA describes collective bargaining and a living wage as foundational; the SI declaration explicitly defends "workers' rights to organize"; DSA centers labor empowerment in its entire program. Scott's repeated, multi-term blocking of every major labor bill the legislature advanced places him in near-total divergence on this axis. Notably, where labor legislation reached his desk in 2024 and 2026, he increasingly chose the let-pass over the veto — a softening of tactics if not of position.

### Healthcare

Scott's healthcare record is thinner than his labor record but pointed: he vetoed **reference-based pricing legislation (S.190, 2026)** on the grounds that savings should apply system-wide rather than to a subset of payers. Universal publicly-funded healthcare, a plank shared by all four organizations (most centrally DSA and SDUSA), has never been advanced to his desk, so his divergence here is partly inferred rather than demonstrated. His stated S.190 reasoning — system-wide affordability — is compatible in rhetoric with universalist social democratic framing while arriving at an anti-interventionist conclusion.

### Climate and Environment

This is arguably his most consequential divergence. Scott vetoed:

- **The Global Warming Solutions Act** (H.688, 2020 — overridden), citing "inefficient spending and long, costly court battles" over its citizen-suit provision
- **The Clean Heat Standard** (H.715, 2022) and its successor, the **Affordable Heat Act** (S.5, 2023 — overridden)
- **The Renewable Energy Standard** requiring 100% renewable power by 2035 (H.289, 2024 — overridden)
- **Neonicotinoid pesticide restrictions** (H.706, 2024 — overridden)
- **Act 250 land-use expansions** repeatedly (S.234, H.687, H.606, and H.926)
- **Community resilience and biodiversity protections** (H.606, 2022)

And he let pass, with objections, the **Climate Superfund Act** (S.259, 2024), making Vermont the first state to seek climate cost recovery from fossil fuel companies — while worrying aloud about "Vermont taking on the oil industry alone."

All four organizations treat climate action as urgent and, in three cases, tie it explicitly to economic restructuring. Scott's consistent rationale is cost to ratepayers and taxpayers — an affordability frame that social democracies (which typically price climate action into public budgets) treat as a solvable distribution problem rather than a reason for inaction. His climate veto record was overridden so frequently in 2023–2024 that the supermajority enacted much of the social-democratic climate agenda over his objection.

### Housing, Childcare, and the Social Wage

This is the most internally complicated category. Scott vetoed:

- **Universal childcare funded by a payroll tax** (H.217, 2023) — "I'm not vetoing childcare. I'm vetoing the payroll tax" — expressing support for the goal while rejecting the funding mechanism central to Act 76
- **Education finance legislation** repeatedly (H.911, H.887 — overridden, over a projected 13.8% property tax increase)
- **Restorative justice expansion** (H.645, 2024 — overridden) and homelessness system overhauls (H.91, H.141, H.489 in 2025)

Yet he also allowed **universal school meals (H.165, 2023)**, **the developmental disability system of care (H.720, 2022)**, and **the Vermont Baby Bond Trust (H.55, 2024)** to become law without his signature. The Baby Bond and universal meals are textbook social-democratic policy instruments; their passage unsigned suggests Scott permits such measures where he cannot identify a constitutional hook or a politically sustainable veto rationale. His veto target in this space is consistently the **funding mechanism** (payroll taxes, property tax increases, broad spending) rather than the benefit itself — a distinction that matters: he obstructs social democracy's *fiscal architecture* more reliably than its *programs*.

### Democracy, Participation, and the Franchise

Scott vetoed:

- **Noncitizen voting charter amendments** for Montpelier and Winooski (H.177, H.277, 2021), invoking "separate and unequal classes of residents" — subsequently overridden
- **Juvenile confidentiality legislation** (S.107, 2021) on transparency grounds

And he let pass unsigned **ranked-choice voting charter approvals for Burlington** (H.744, 2022; H.508, 2023), stating his belief that "one person should get one vote, and candidates who get the most votes should win elections."

His reasoning style here is notable: he justifies franchise restriction in *egalitarian* vocabulary. The DSA platform explicitly calls for a new democratic constitution with proportional representation; SI calls for "advanced participatory democracy." Scott's instinct runs majoritarian and first-past-the-post. Interestingly, his open-government justifications (H.649 Truth and Reconciliation Commission let-pass over closed deliberations, H.1 Ethics Commission let-pass) display a transparency commitment that a liberal democrat could applaud but that none of the four organizations foreground.

### Criminal Justice, Guns, and Civil Liberties

Against expectation from a Republican governor, this is where Scott's record converges most with progressive and even social-democratic positions. He let pass:

- **Statewide use-of-force standards** with de-escalation training mandates (S.119, 2020)
- **Firearm safe storage, expanded extreme-risk orders, and a 72-hour waiting period** (H.230, 2023) — deferring his constitutional objections to the courts
- **The ghost gun ban** (S.209, 2024)
- **Enabled civil suits against government officials for federal constitutional rights violations** (H.849, Act 87, 2026) . This is permitted, not advocated: Scott allowed it to become law while stating it risked giving Vermonters "false hope" that it would survive court challenges, permitting it only after the Attorney General advised it was defensible. It registers as passive tolerance, not championing — the legislature and AG, not the governor, deserve the credit.

None of the four organizations treat gun regulation as a defining plank, but the civil-liberties and police-accountability dimensions here are consistent with SDUSA's and SI's rights frameworks. This category demonstrates that Scott's let-passes are not reserved for low-stakes bills — he declined to veto genuinely ambitious public-safety legislation at the height of national controversy over each measure.

### Separation of Powers as Governing Ideology

Across all categories, Scott's most frequently stated veto rationale is structural: legislators "cannot mandate what he does or does not include in his budget proposal" (H.817, 2026; H.219, 2017; S.39 in substance). For social democrats, who favor an empowered, activist legislature executing an explicit popular mandate, this executive-centric theory of budgeting is itself an ideological position — one that systematically disadvantages new social programs, which require appropriations. Whatever else may be said of it, this is the mechanism through which much of his divergence from all four organizations operates.

## Alignment with the Four Organizations

### Democratic Socialists of America (DSA)

**Alignment: Weak.** Scott's record contradicts nearly every DSA economic plank: he vetoed the minimum wage twice, paid family leave twice, collective bargaining measures, and the labor-backed childcare funding mechanism, while blocking the core DSA climate agenda (Clean Heat Standard, Renewable Energy Standard). Convergence is limited to the let-passed gun-safety and police-accountability bills and to rhetorical affordability concern for low-income ratepayers. He has never used class-struggle or structural-critique framing, and his constant theme — restraining state spending growth — is antithetical to DSA's expansion of the public sector.

### Social Democrats of America (SDA)

**Alignment: Weak, with procedural nuance.** SDA's platform centers universal healthcare, education, reproductive rights, the right to unionize, and government ensuring a dignified life. Scott's labor vetoes alone place him in divergence, and universal healthcare was never advanced to him. The partial exception is reproductive rights: Proposition 3 codifying reproductive freedom proceeded in Vermont without his obstruction — a genuine, if passive, convergence with SDA's explicitly pro-abortion platform. His open-government let-passes (H.649, H.1) also faintly echo SDA's civil-liberties language, though from a different philosophical starting point.

### Socialist International (SI)

**Alignment: Weak.** The SI declaration's core commitments — public ownership and democratic management of key resources, climate justice, participatory democracy, international solidarity — map poorly onto Scott's record. His climate vetoes (including the Global Warming Solutions Act, overridden) contradict the climate-justice plank; his noncitizen-voting vetoes and RCV refusals contradict the participatory-democracy plank; his consistent preference for private, deregulated development over Act 250 protections contradicts any notion of social ownership. The closest behavioral convergence with SI's human-rights framing is his let-pass of H.849 (Act 87, 2026), enabling civil suits against government officials for federal constitutional violations — but it should be weighted cautiously: Scott permitted it reluctantly, stating it risked giving Vermonters "false hope" it would survive court challenge, and allowed it only after the Attorney General advised it was defensible. It is a permission, not an endorsement.

### Social Democrats USA (SDUSA)

**Alignment: Weak-to-moderate — the closest of the four.** SDUSA is the most reformist and institutionally conservative of the four frameworks, favoring a mixed economy, NATO-aligned multilateralism, and incremental expansion. Three elements of Scott's record partially resonate: (1) his pro-development critique of Act 250 as a housing obstacle shares surface logic with SDUSA's affordable-housing emphasis, though SDUSA would pursue subsidy and construction rather than deregulation alone; (2) his transparency and open-government let-passes reflect the small-d democratic proceduralism SDUSA values; (3) his fiscal caution is philosophically compatible with mixed-economy pragmatism in the abstract — though in practice he deployed it against the payroll tax, minimum wage, and climate investment that SDUSA's platform requires. Even on this most sympathetic yardstick, the substance of his record diverges: SDUSA supports a living federal minimum wage, union power, and Green New Deal-type programs, all of which Scott blocked.

## Key Observations

**1. Scott's veto pattern is fiscally targeted, not ideologically blanket.** He most reliably vetoed legislation that created new taxes, expanded spending bases, or constrained his budgetary authority — the payroll tax, property tax increases, minimum wage, reference-based pricing. He let pass a striking number of social programs that arrived pre-funded or whose veto rationale could be deferred to the courts. Social democrats should read this as: the programs Scott tolerated were those the legislature succeeded in funding anyway.

**2. The overrides tell a second story.** At least ten of Scott's vetoes were overridden by the legislature's supermajority, including the entire 2023–2024 climate agenda and both noncitizen-voting charter amendments. Much of what a social democrat might credit to Vermont's policy environment was enacted *against* Scott, not through him — which itself distinguishes the general-election choice from the state's policy trajectory.

**3. His egalitarian vocabulary is used for anti-expansionary ends.** Noncitizen voting was rejected in the name of preventing "separate and unequal classes of residents"; ranked-choice voting in the name of "one person should get one vote"; climate vetoes in the name of protecting "Vermonters who can least afford" transition costs. Analysts should engage the arguments as made rather than dismiss them as pretext — but note that in each case, the egalitarian framing was deployed against franchise expansion or public investment.

**4. The sharpest behavioral divergences, in order:** labor rights (five-plus direct vetoes across three terms), climate program-building (seven-plus vetoes, mostly overridden), franchise expansion (three vetoes, overridden), and the fiscal architecture of the social wage (childcare payroll tax, education finance). The most surprising convergence area is public safety and civil liberties, where his let-pass record is genuinely progressive relative to his party's national posture.

**5. The record is incomplete in ways that flatter no one.** Silence dominates on immigration (until the H.849 let-pass, contextualized by ICE conduct in Vermont), foreign affairs (outside gubernatorial scope), and any question of public ownership. Readers should resist scoring him on domains where no legislation reached his desk.

## Addendum: Disclaimer on Data Uncertainty

*Note: This analysis inherits the data limitations of its source compilations. Entries where Scott's stated reasoning was not recoverable (several 2018 and 2022 vetoes, multiple let-pass letters) are excluded from interpretation rather than assumed neutral; readers should verify any individual bill claim against the Vermont Legislature's official trackers before citing. The approximate totals (~66 vetoes, ~28 let-passes) reflect the unresolved ambiguities described in the source documents, including one unidentified 2021–2022 veto and the historically ambiguous H.910. The thematic groupings and alignment ratings are interpretive judgments, not neutral facts, and are offered as an analytical lens rather than a definitive classification.*
